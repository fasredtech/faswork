# FASWORK Maintenance Lifecycle

## Overview

FASWORK is a maintenance lifecycle management platform being developed by FASRED Technologies Ltd. It is designed to help organisations manage assets, respond to maintenance needs, coordinate work, control resources and costs, verify completed work, and improve operational reliability.

FASWORK treats maintenance as a connected lifecycle rather than a collection of isolated work orders. The lifecycle connects asset condition, problem discovery, diagnosis, risk assessment, planning, authorisation, resource readiness, execution, verification, acceptance, return to service, reconciliation, and continuous improvement.

The exact workflow depends on the type of work, its risk, the organisation's operating requirements, and the applicable procedures.

## The Maintenance Lifecycle

### 1. Asset and Operational Context

Establish the context needed to manage maintenance activities, including:

- Asset identity, classification, ownership, and history.
- Asset location and organisational responsibility.
- Equipment, facilities, infrastructure, and associated components.
- Relevant warranties, contracts, documentation, and service history.
- Asset availability and operational importance.

Accurate asset context helps organisations understand what needs attention, where it is located, and how maintenance may affect operations.

### 2. Problem Discovery and Reporting

Maintenance needs may arise from reported faults, service requests, routine inspections, observed deterioration, recurring failures, alarms, or condition-monitoring systems where available.

The process should preserve the reported information and its source so that the original concern can be traced throughout the maintenance lifecycle.

### 3. Triage and Diagnosis

Reported issues are assessed to determine their nature, urgency, potential consequences, and appropriate next steps.

This stage may involve:

- Reviewing the reported symptoms and available evidence.
- Identifying duplicate or related reports.
- Assessing operational impact and urgency.
- Investigating possible causes.
- Recording uncertainty where the cause is not yet known.
- Determining whether further inspection or specialist assessment is required.

A reported symptom should not automatically be treated as a confirmed diagnosis.

### 4. Risk Assessment and Maintenance Decision

The organisation determines the appropriate response based on the condition of the asset, the risk involved, operational requirements, and available evidence.

Possible decisions include monitoring, further investigation, corrective maintenance, preventive maintenance, emergency intervention, restriction of use, or removal from service.

The process should support proportionate controls rather than imposing the same approval and verification requirements on every maintenance task.

### 5. Planning and Work Preparation

Approved maintenance activities are prepared for execution.

Depending on the work, planning may cover:

- Work scope and objectives.
- Job plans, procedures, and required tasks.
- Estimated duration and scheduling.
- Required skills, labour, tools, equipment, and materials.
- Safety precautions and applicable permits.
- Dependencies, operational interruptions, and coordination.
- Expected costs and verification requirements.

Planning should make the intended work, responsibilities, and completion criteria clear before execution begins.

### 6. Authorisation and Resource Readiness

Where required, maintenance work is reviewed and authorised by the appropriate personnel.

Readiness may depend on approval, staff availability, contractor arrangements, materials, tools, access, operational coordination, and safety preparations.

An authorised work order does not automatically mean that the work is ready to begin. Outstanding prerequisites must be handled according to the applicable workflow.

### 7. Maintenance Execution

Maintenance personnel perform the authorised work and record relevant activities and evidence.

Records may include work performed, observations, measurements, materials used, labour, delays, unexpected findings, additional defects, and deviations from the original plan.

Where the work changes in scope or reveals a new risk, the process should support appropriate reassessment and authorisation.

### 8. Technical Verification and Testing

The completed work is checked against its defined requirements.

Depending on the maintenance activity, this may include inspection, functional testing, measurements, calibration, quality checks, specialist review, or other documented verification.

Required checks must be evaluated against their actual results. Missing evidence must not be treated as a successful test.

Where verification fails, the asset or work must follow the appropriate corrective or escalation process.

### 9. Acceptance and Return to Service

Technical verification and operational acceptance are related but distinct activities.

Where applicable, authorised personnel confirm that the work meets its acceptance criteria and that the asset can safely return to its intended service.

An asset should not be marked as available merely because the work order has been marked completed. Outstanding defects, failed tests, unresolved safety conditions, or missing mandatory approvals must be addressed through the applicable controls.

FASWORK is intended to support accountable decisions; it does not replace qualified human judgement or authoritative safety procedures.

### 10. Materials, Inventory, and Cost Reconciliation

Maintenance records should be reconciled with the resources and financial commitments associated with the work.

This may include:

- Materials requested, approved, purchased, received, issued, consumed, or returned.
- Tools and equipment used.
- Labour and contractor costs.
- Purchase orders, invoices, and payment status.
- Budget commitments and actual expenditure.
- Variances, outstanding items, and financial adjustments.

Related events must remain distinguishable. For example, placing an order does not prove receipt, and approving an invoice does not prove payment.

### 11. Closure and Handover

Administrative closure brings the maintenance record together after the applicable work, verification, acceptance, and reconciliation requirements have been satisfied.

Depending on the work, closure information may include:

- Work performed and final findings.
- Verification and acceptance evidence.
- Asset service status.
- Materials and cost records.
- Outstanding follow-up actions.
- Relevant documentation and audit history.
- Handover information for operators or future maintenance teams.

A status change alone should not substitute for required completion evidence.

### 12. Post-Maintenance Monitoring and Improvement

The lifecycle continues after closure where follow-up is appropriate.

Organisations can use maintenance history and operational data to identify recurring faults, assess repair effectiveness, review expenditure, improve maintenance schedules, and identify opportunities to reduce downtime.

Potential measures include asset availability, repeat failures, maintenance backlog, response and repair times, planned versus unplanned work, cost trends, and compliance with defined maintenance requirements.

Reliable analysis depends on clear metric definitions and trustworthy source data. Missing information should not silently be interpreted as zero.

## Maintenance Strategies

FASWORK is intended to support different maintenance approaches according to organisational needs, asset types, risk, and operating conditions.

These may include:

- Corrective and emergency maintenance.
- Preventive and routine maintenance.
- Condition-based and predictive maintenance.
- Inspections and statutory maintenance.
- Reliability-focused maintenance.
- Shutdowns, turnarounds, and overhauls.
- Warranty and contractor-managed maintenance.
- Calibration, testing, installation, commissioning, and decommissioning.

The availability of any particular strategy or integration depends on the product's implementation and release status.

## Designed for Different Organisations

FASWORK is intended to serve organisations ranging from small workshops and single-site institutions to complex, multi-location enterprises and public-sector organisations.

Potential operating environments include healthcare, education, manufacturing, construction, facilities management, energy, utilities, transport, logistics, agriculture, government, hospitality, commercial property, and research facilities.

Organisations should be able to configure appropriate structures, terminology, responsibilities, permissions, workflows, and reporting while maintaining suitable separation between tenants and locations.

## Governance, Safety, and Accountability

The maintenance lifecycle is intended to support:

- Traceable records and accountable decisions.
- Appropriate role-based permissions and organisational scope.
- Evidence-backed work completion and verification.
- Controlled approval and state transitions.
- Visibility into outstanding risks and follow-up actions.
- Separation of requested, authorised, executed, verified, and accepted work.
- Reliable records for review, reporting, and continuous improvement.

FASWORK supports maintenance management and decision-making. It does not independently guarantee equipment safety, regulatory compliance, or the correctness of professional engineering decisions. Organisations remain responsible for applicable standards, qualified personnel, and authorised procedures.
